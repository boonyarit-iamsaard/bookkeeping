SAA-C03: identity and organizations

The exam view of the ground ticket 01 covered: who gets access through what, how AWS decides a request, and what Organizations and the root user do. It sits in domain 1, Design Secure Architectures, the largest domain at 30% of scored content. Each part uses this project's own setup as the example; the operational detail is in AWS foundation lessons.

Who needs access, and through what

Exam scenarios describe who or what needs access, and the right answer nearly always swaps long-term keys for temporary credentials.

Policy types

Only two kinds of policy grant anything. Every other kind sets a ceiling, and an action must fit under every ceiling that applies.

Policy Attached to Effect Grants?
Identity-based An IAM user, group or role What that principal may do Yes
Resource-based A resource: S3 bucket policy, KMS key policy, SQS, SNS, Lambda Who may act on that resource, named in Principal. It is how one account grants access to another. Yes
Role trust policy An IAM role The resource-based policy of a role: who may assume it Only sts:AssumeRole
Permissions boundary An IAM user or role The most its identity-based policies can allow. Use one to let developers create roles without escalating their own access. No
Session policy One AssumeRole or federation session Narrows that one session No
SCP The org root, an OU or an account The most any IAM user or role in a member account can do, including that account's root user. It does not affect the management account or service-linked roles. No
RCP newer The org root, an OU or an account The most anyone, even another account, can do to resources in a member account, for services such as S3, STS, KMS, SQS and Secrets Manager No
Tag, backup and AI opt-out policies The org root, an OU or an account Management settings, not permissions. A backup policy rolls an AWS Backup plan out across accounts. Not a permission

How AWS decides a request

Everything starts denied. These are the gates, simplified to the depth the exam tests; the IAM guide's flowchart has edge cases beyond it.

  1. Any explicit Deny? In any policy that applies, of any type. An explicit deny always wins. yes → denied
  2. RCPs allow it? Only for resources in member accounts. no → denied
  3. SCPs allow it at every level? The org root, each OU on the path, and the account. Only for principals in member accounts. no → denied
  4. Identity-based or resource-based allow? Within one account, either is enough. Across accounts, both are needed: the caller's identity policy and the resource's policy. neither → denied
  5. Permissions boundary and session policy? Where present, each must also allow the action. no → denied
  6. Every gate passed The request runs. allowed
allowed = no explicit deny
  and RCPs allow and SCPs allow at every level
  and (identity policy allows or resource policy allows)
  and boundary allows and session policy allows

Worked through this project

The owner tries to take backups out of the Organization

Signed in through Identity Center as AdministratorAccess, the owner calls organizations:LeaveOrganization in backups. The identity policy allows it, but the default SCP's explicit deny stops it at gate 1. Administrator access in a member account is still capped by SCPs.

The same role in the management account

SCPs never reach the management account, so no SCP could stop the owner there. That is why the management account holds no workloads: nothing in the Organization can restrict it.

The uploader writes, then tries to read

s3:PutObject on daily/… over HTTPS: there is no explicit deny, FullAWSAccess passes the SCP gate, and the identity policy allows it, so it runs. s3:GetObject on the same key: nothing allows it, so it is an implicit deny. No deny statement is needed to keep the uploader out.

The uploader sends over plain HTTP

The bucket policy denies any request where aws:SecureTransport is false. That explicit deny beats the identity policy's allow at gate 1.

The owner deletes a locked backup version

IAM allows it, and S3 still refuses. Object Lock in compliance mode is a property of the object version, not a policy, and not even root can shorten it. Governance mode, by contrast, lets anyone holding s3:BypassGovernanceRetention override it. Not every refusal comes from IAM.

Organizations

Fact What to remember
All features vs consolidated billing Consolidated billing gives one bill and nothing else. SCPs, RCPs, other policy types, trusted access, delegated administrators and centralized root access all need all features. Organizations itself costs nothing.
Consolidated billing savings Usage across accounts is combined to reach volume pricing tiers, and Reserved Instance and Savings Plans discounts are shared across accounts unless sharing is turned off. This is a common answer in the cost-optimization domain.
Management account Pays the bill, owns the Organization, and cannot be restricted by SCPs. Keep workloads out of it, and hand service administration to a member account as a delegated administrator.
OUs and inheritance An SCP on an OU applies to everything under it. Denies add up down the path; an allow must exist at every level. With the default FullAWSAccess in place you write deny lists; removing it means writing allow lists. Removing it with nothing in its place blocks everything below.
Why use several accounts To separate blast radius, billing, security boundaries and service quotas. Here, backups is isolated from the planned sandbox for exam practice.
Control Tower The answer when a question asks for a multi-account landing zone with built-in guardrails and an account factory, set up quickly. It is built on Organizations, Identity Center, SCPs, Config and CloudTrail. Two accounts did not need it.
Org-wide services An organization trail in CloudTrail logs every account in one place. AWS RAM shares resources such as subnets or a Transit Gateway with other accounts. Both need trusted access.

IAM Identity Center

  • The successor to AWS SSO, and AWS's recommended way to give people access. It is free. An organization instance covers every account and lives in one home Region.
  • A permission set is a template. Assigning it to a user or group in an account provisions an AWSReservedSSO_… role there. Session duration is 1 to 12 hours, 1 by default.
  • Identity sources: its own directory (used here), Active Directory, or an external SAML 2.0 IdP such as Entra ID or Okta, with SCIM to provision users.
  • Attribute-based access control: user attributes arrive as session tags, so a single permission set can scope access by tag, for example by team or cost center.
  • Its administration can be delegated to a member account, keeping people out of the management account.

The root user

  • Turn on MFA, create no access keys, keep the contact details current, and never use it for daily work.
  • Root-only tasks: changing a standalone account's root email or password, and closing a standalone account; restoring IAM permissions after an admin lockout; activating IAM access to Billing; enabling MFA Delete on an S3 bucket; editing or deleting a bucket or SQS policy that denies every principal; registering as a seller in the Reserved Instance Marketplace.
  • SCPs restrict a member account's root user; nothing restricts the management account's root.
  • Centralized root access newer removes member accounts' root credentials and runs single-task root sessions with sts:AssumeRoot: auditing, unlocking a deny-all bucket or queue policy, deleting credentials, or allowing password recovery. It is new enough to appear rarely on the exam; the principles above are what get tested.

Trap statements

Decide true or false, then open each one to check.

An SCP attached to the org root also limits the management account.

False. SCPs never affect the management account, wherever they are attached.

An SCP that allows s3:* gives a user S3 access.

False. SCPs only set a ceiling. The user still needs an identity-based or resource-based allow.

An SCP can stop a member account's root user.

True. That is one of the main reasons to use SCPs.

SCPs restrict service-linked roles.

False. Service-linked roles are exempt so that AWS services keep working.

Detaching FullAWSAccess from an OU, with nothing else attached, changes nothing.

False. No SCP allows anything there any more, so every action in accounts under that OU is denied.

An organization with consolidated billing only can use SCPs.

False. SCPs need all features.

A permissions boundary can give a role extra permissions.

False. A boundary only caps; the effective permissions are where the identity policy and the boundary overlap.

Within one account, an S3 bucket policy alone can let a role read the bucket.

True. In the same account, an allow in either the identity policy or the resource policy is enough. Across accounts, both sides must allow it.

Mobile app users should sign in through IAM Identity Center.

False. Identity Center is for the workforce. App customers use Cognito.

The safe way to give a vendor access is an IAM user with keys you rotate.

False. Use a cross-account role whose trust policy requires an external ID.

An EC2 application should read its keys from an encrypted environment variable.

False. Attach an instance profile; the SDK gets temporary, rotated credentials from instance metadata.

Root with MFA is fine for daily admin work.

False. MFA is required, but root is for root-only tasks. Daily work goes through Identity Center.

Enabling MFA Delete on a bucket needs the root user.

True. It is one of the few root-only tasks, and it is done through the API or CLI, not the console.

Under Object Lock compliance mode, an admin with s3:BypassGovernanceRetention can delete a locked version early.

False. That permission only overrides governance mode. Under compliance mode nobody can, not even root, until the retention period ends.

Linked accounts in one organization share Reserved Instance discounts.

True, by default. The management account can turn off sharing.
